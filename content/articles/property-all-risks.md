# Understanding Property All Risks

“All risks” is a starting point for reading the wording, not a promise that every loss is insured.

## What does Property All Risks mean?
Property all risks is a form of property insurance whose protection is defined by its wording, schedule, exclusions and conditions. The name does not mean that every cause of loss, every asset or every financial consequence is covered.

The useful question is therefore not simply whether a policy is called “all risks”. It is what property is insured, against which types of loss, on what basis and subject to which conditions. Two policies with similar names can have important differences.

## Start with the insured property
Read the schedule alongside the wording. Identify the property described, the location and the person or business whose interest is insured. A building, tenant’s improvements, equipment, stock and personal contents may raise different questions. Do not assume that naming a premises automatically includes everything inside it.

Explain how the property is used and whether there are changes such as renovations, vacancy or new activities. Ask which changes need to be notified and how that notification should be made.

## Look at values as well as limits
The sum insured should be considered against the policy’s valuation basis. Reinstatement, replacement and other bases are not interchangeable. A market value figure may not answer the insurer’s question about the cost of replacing an asset.

Ask whether the policy contains an underinsurance or average condition and how it could affect a claim. Review separate sub-limits and any excess that you would need to bear. A high headline sum insured does not remove a smaller limit applying to a particular category.

## Read the exclusions and conditions
Exclusions identify losses or circumstances the policy does not insure. Conditions may address safeguards, notification, maintenance or other responsibilities. Their practical effect depends on the actual wording and circumstances.

Ask specifically about the exposures that concern you. If a particular event, item or consequence matters to your business, seek an explanation of how the wording treats it. Avoid relying on a general product label or a short sales summary.

## Five questions for your review
- What property and interests are included in the schedule?
- What valuation basis and sums insured should be used?
- Which exclusions or sub-limits matter most to these assets?
- What excesses and ongoing conditions apply?
- Which changes and losses must be notified, and through which channel?

## Bring the policy into the conversation
For an existing policy review, bring the schedule, wording, relevant endorsements and an outline of changes since the last renewal. These provide a more useful basis for comparison than premium alone.
